Tutorial #2 — Basic Brain Decoding on EEG Data

Train and test a deep-learning model on EEG in the classic setup: trials of brain data, each with a label (Left Hand, Right Hand, Feet, Tongue). We follow the braindecode BCIC IV 2a example end to end, and explain every step.

What this tutorial covers

Decoding EEG is a pipeline. We walk it one stage at a time, and you run each stage yourself:

  • Part 1 — Loading and preparing the data: load the dataset → preprocess it → cut it into labeled windows → split into training and test.
  • Part 2 — Creating a model: pick a deep network sized to the data.
  • Part 3 — Model training (and training for longer).
  • Part 4 — Plot the training curves.
  • Part 5 — Plot a confusion matrix to see which movements it confuses.
About the dataset · BCI Competition IV 2a

What it is: real EEG from 9 people who repeatedly imagined moving one of four body parts. Recorded by the Graz BCI Lab for the 2008 BCI Competition IV, it is the reference benchmark for motor-imagery decoding. In MOABB/braindecode it loads under the id BNCI2014_001.

LabelImagined movement
0Left hand
1Right hand
2Both feet
3Tongue
  • 9 subjects, each recorded in 2 sessions on different days — one for training, one for testing.
  • 288 trials per session, 72 per class.
  • 22 EEG electrodes over sensorimotor cortex, 250 Hz (4 ms/sample).
  • Each ~7.5 s trial: a cue at t = 2 s tells the person which movement to imagine; they sustain it until t = 6 s.
Jetson note: the first time you pick a subject the data downloads (hundreds of MB) and is preprocessed, so the first load takes a minute or two. Needs moabb in the container (pip install moabb).
Part 1 · Loading and preparing the data
Step 1 · Loading the dataset

braindecode wraps MOABB — a library that knows how to download and read dozens of public EEG datasets — in a MOABBDataset. We ask it for BNCI2014_001, one subject at a time. That gives us the subject's two continuous recordings (train day + test day), still raw.

Everything else about the recording is fixed by the experiment — the only choice you have is which of the 9 people to decode.

Step 2 · Preprocessing

Raw EEG is messy. Before a model sees it, we clean and normalize it — the exact four steps from the braindecode example, applied to the continuous signal:

  • Keep only the EEG channels. The recording also has EOG (eye-movement) sensors; those aren't brain signal, so we drop them, leaving the 22 EEG channels.
  • Convert volts → microvolts (×1,000,000). EEG is tiny; working in µV keeps the numbers in a friendly range.
  • Band-pass filter 4–38 Hz. Motor imagery lives in the mu (~8–12 Hz) and beta (~13–30 Hz) rhythms. Filtering to 4–38 Hz keeps those and throws away slow drifts and high-frequency noise.
  • Exponential moving standardization. Each channel is continuously re-centered and re-scaled as the recording plays, so no channel dominates just because it happens to have a bigger amplitude. (Parameters from the example: factor_new = 1e-3, init_block_size = 1000.)

This runs automatically when you load a subject — the numbers you see in Step 3 are already preprocessed.

Step 3 · Extracting compute windows

A model can't train on one long recording — it needs many short, equal-length, labeled examples. So we cut the signal into trial windows: one window per cue, starting 0.5 s before the cue and running 4.5 s (1,125 samples) — using braindecode's create_windows_from_events. Each window is a grid of 22 channels × 1,125 samples with one of the 4 labels. Stack them and you get X (the clips) and y (the labels).

First load downloads the subject and can take a minute or two.

Step 4 · Splitting into training and test sets

To measure learning honestly, the model must be scored on data it never trained on. BCI IV 2a makes this easy and strict: it was recorded on two different days, so we simply split by session —

  • Train on session 0train (288 trials, day 1).
  • Test on session 1test (288 trials, day 2), held out.

Training on one day and testing on another is much harder than a random split — the model has to generalize across days — which is exactly what makes this a respected benchmark.

Part 2 · Creating a model
Step 5 · Pick a model & settings

braindecode ships EEG-specific deep networks. The example uses ShallowFBCSPNet — a small, fast convolutional network (~47k parameters) built to mimic the classic FBCSP pipeline: it learns frequency-band filters, then how much power each band carries, then maps that to the 4 classes. It's the recommended starting point; Deep4Net is bigger and EEGNet is more compact.

Defaults match the reference (batch 64, learning rate 0.000625).

Part 3 · Model training
Step 6 · Train — and train for longer

Training shows the model the 0train clips over and over (one epoch = one full pass) and nudges its weights each batch to predict the labels better. Then we score it on the untouched 1test session. Training for longer — more epochs — is how you climb toward the paper's ~68%; a handful of epochs will land far lower. (Four classes → 25% is chance.)

Part 4 · Plot the training curves
Step 7 · Training curves

These show how the run went, epoch by epoch. Loss (blue) should trend down as the model fits the training clips. Validation accuracy (green) should trend up. If loss keeps falling but accuracy stops rising, the model is starting to memorize rather than generalize — a sign to stop, or to use more data.

Part 5 · Plot a confusion matrix
Step 8 · Confusion matrix

Accuracy is one number; a confusion matrix shows the whole story. Each row is the true movement, each column is what the model predicted. The green diagonal is correct answers; red off-diagonal cells are mistakes — and they tell you which movements get mixed up (hands vs. feet vs. tongue), which raw accuracy can't. A perfect model is all green on the diagonal.
